\title{Do Tabular Foundation Models Pay Off for Churn?\\
A Profit-, Calibration- and Tuning-Aware Re-evaluation}
\author{Ismail Hossain Polas\\ Jahangirnagar University\\ \texttt{ismailhossainpolash@gmail.com}}
\date{October 28, 2026}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Tabular foundation models (\tfms) predict from a labelled context in a single forward pass. A recent benchmark
reported that they decisively outperform tree ensembles for customer churn, but it compared them with untuned
gradient-boosted trees (GBDTs) and scored only ranking metrics, while a retention campaign is judged in money. We
re-evaluate TabPFN-2.5, TabPFN-3.5, TabPFN-3.5-Fast and TabICL v2 against Optuna-tuned GBDTs on five public telecom
churn datasets over \nSeeds{} seeded splits. We separate three questions: whether a model ranks churners well, whether
that ranking earns more expected maximum profit (EMP), and whether its probabilities can drive a threshold policy. With
a split-aware test and Holm correction over \nPrimary{} pre-declared comparisons, TabPFN-3.5 earns significantly more
EMP than the best tuned GBDT on Cell2Cell (\empPFNThreeCellTwoCellAbs\%) and Iranian (\empPFNThreeIranianAbs\%). On
Orange it wins in all \nSeeds{} splits (\empPFNThreeOrangeAbs\%), but the gain does not survive correction. TabICL v2,
the earlier benchmark's leader, is significant only on Iranian and underpredicts Orange churn by
\citlICLOrangeAbs{} points on a \rateOrange\% base rate. On the datasets and splits where we tested them, a fivefold larger
tuning budget and a duplicate-aware split leave these conclusions unchanged. Retuning the baseline cuts the earlier headline gap from \replICLDefaultAbs{} to
\replICLTunedAbs{} PR-AUC points without closing it. The licence of TabPFN-3.5's weights, not scoring cost, is what
stands between its gains and production use.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Churn models exist to choose whom a retention campaign contacts. A model is useful to an operator to the extent that
the customers it ranks highest are the ones worth an incentive, so the natural yardstick is campaign profit rather than
classification accuracy. Verbraken et al.\ made this precise with the expected maximum profit (EMP) criterion
\cite{verbraken2013emp}, and later work showed that selecting churn models by AUC instead of profit leaves money on the
table \cite{verbeke2012newinsights,hoppner2020proftree,manzoor2025eprofits}.

Tabular foundation models change what a churn model is. TabPFN \cite{hollmann2025tabpfn,priorlabs2025tabpfn25,priorlabs2026tabpfn35}
and TabICL \cite{qu2025tabicl,qu2026tabiclv2} are pretrained on synthetic tables and predict a new customer by
conditioning on the labelled training set as context, with no fitting and no hyperparameter search. On general
benchmarks they now lead or match tuned GBDTs \cite{erickson2025tabarena,qu2026tabiclv2,priorlabs2026tabpfn35}. For
churn specifically, Seyedzadeh and Karimi \cite{seyedzadeh2026churn} benchmarked six \tfms on nine Kaggle datasets.
They concluded that \tfms ``collectively and decisively outperform'' classical, deep and tree-ensemble models, with
TabICL v2 ahead of XGBoost by up to 9.23 PR-AUC points.

That study was the first to bring \tfms to churn, and it leaves three questions open. Its XGBoost and random forest
used library-default hyperparameters \cite[Section~3.2]{seyedzadeh2026churn}, so part of the reported gap may be a tuning
gap. Its metrics were F1, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC and log loss; its financial estimate scaled the PR-AUC gap by a retention rate
rather than evaluating a campaign. Finally, its own discussion reads TabICL v2's lead as ``operational \ldots\ rather
than as a statistically decisive metric lead'' \cite[Section~4.2]{seyedzadeh2026churn}, which leaves the size of the lead
unsettled.

We argue that the practical value of a \tfm for churn depends on the model generation, the dataset, the decision policy
and the deployment terms, not on membership of the model class. We test this with tuned baselines and an explicit
campaign model. Four \tfms (TabPFN-2.5, TabPFN-3.5, TabPFN-3.5-Fast, TabICL v2) face Optuna-tuned LightGBM, XGBoost and
CatBoost, an explainable boosting machine and logistic regression on five public telecom datasets, three of them from
real operators, over \nSeeds{} seeded splits. Our inference treats the split as a source of variation and corrects for
multiple comparisons. The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A tuned, profit-aware comparison.} TabPFN-3.5 earns significantly more EMP than the best tuned GBDT on
  Cell2Cell and Iranian after Holm correction, wins in every split on Orange without surviving correction, and is
  never worse beyond noise. TabICL v2 and TabPFN-2.5 are significant only on Iranian. On the two fictional datasets,
  Telco and Maven, no difference survives correction (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}).
\item \textbf{Ranking, profit and probabilities kept apart.} Profit metrics rerank models only when they are tied
  (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}). Calibration matters for one model on one dataset: raw TabICL v2 loses
  \regICLOrangeRaw\% of attainable campaign profit on Orange at a fixed threshold (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
\item \textbf{Robustness to the obvious objections.} A balanced context hurts rather than helps when the training set
  fits (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}). A fivefold larger tuning budget (on the three real-operator datasets) and a split that
  keeps duplicates without leaking them (on the three datasets that had any) change no conclusion within the five
  splits each check covers (Section~\ref{sec:sens}).
\item \textbf{A bounded replication.} On the earlier benchmark's headline dataset, tuning removes about a third of
  TabICL v2's PR-AUC lead, and the rest survives (Section~\ref{sec:rq5}).
\item \textbf{Deployment terms.} Cached scoring is cheap; the TabPFN weights' licence, which excludes production use, is
  the binding constraint (Section~\ref{sec:rq6}).
\end{enumerate}
The code, every per-split prediction, and the generator and audit for every number in this paper are released
(Section~\ref{sec:availability}).

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Profit-aware churn evaluation.}
Verbraken et al.\ defined EMP as the expected best campaign profit when the probability that a contacted churner
accepts the offer is uncertain and Beta distributed \cite{verbraken2013emp}. Verbeke et al.\ used profit to compare
churn classifiers in telecom \cite{verbeke2012newinsights}, and Höppner et al.\ optimised EMP directly when growing
decision trees \cite{hoppner2020proftree}. Manzoor et al.\ proposed e-Profits, which replaces EMP's population-level
customer value with a per-customer value derived from Kaplan--Meier retention, and found that it reranks six classical
models on IBM Telco and Maven \cite{manzoor2025eprofits}. None of these studies includes a foundation model.

\paragraph{Tabular foundation models.}
TabPFN v2 \cite{hollmann2025tabpfn} established prior-fitted transformers for tables up to roughly 10k rows;
TabPFN-2.5 extended this to 50k rows \cite{priorlabs2025tabpfn25}, and TabPFN-3 and 3.5 to a million rows with a cached
context for fast scoring \cite{priorlabs2026tabpfn3,priorlabs2026tabpfn35}. TabICL separates column and row attention to
scale in-context learning \cite{qu2025tabicl}; TabICL v2 reports beating a tuned and ensembled RealTabPFN-2.5 on TabArena
and TALENT without tuning \cite{qu2026tabiclv2}. On TabArena, GBDTs remain strong on larger datasets while \tfms excel on
small ones \cite{erickson2025tabarena}, and TabPrep shows that feature engineering, absent from most benchmarks,
changes these comparisons \cite{tschalzev2026tabprep}. TabPFN's predictive probabilities are reported to be well
calibrated out of the box \cite{tanna2025tabtune}, while ensembling \tfms can damage calibration
\cite{tanna2026ensembling}.

\paragraph{\tfms in business prediction.}
The closest analogue to our study is the credit-risk review of Baesens et al.\ \cite{baesens2026creditrisk}. Across
14 probability-of-default datasets TabPFN-3 ranks first, but its margin over the strongest GBDT is narrow and ``driven
by the latest TabPFN and TabICL generations rather than by the model class as a whole''. TabPFN-3 also underpredicts
the mean default rate. Tanna et al.\ found that, under heavy imbalance, building a class-balanced context adds 3 to 4 AUC
points over uniform sampling for subsampled contexts of 1k to 50k rows \cite{tanna2026resampling}; V4FinBench reports
a similar benefit of imbalance-aware context construction for bankruptcy prediction \cite{kostrzewa2026v4finbench}.
For churn, the only \tfm benchmark is \cite{seyedzadeh2026churn}. Table~\ref{tab:gap} sets the closest studies against
ours.

\section{Benchmark Design}

\subsection{Datasets}
Table~\ref{tab:data} lists the six datasets. Five form the main benchmark. Cell2Cell, Iranian and Orange come from
real operators: Cell2Cell from a US wireless carrier \cite{cell2cell}, Iranian from an Iranian operator through the UCI
repository \cite{iranian}, and Orange from the KDD Cup 2009 churn task \cite{guyon2009kddcup}, which we load from
OpenML (dataset 1112). IBM Telco \cite{telco} and Maven \cite{maven} are fictional teaching datasets; we keep them
because prior churn studies use them, including both \cite{manzoor2025eprofits} and \cite{seyedzadeh2026churn}, and
because a gap between real and fictional data would itself be informative. E-Commerce \cite{ecommerce}, the dataset on
which \cite{seyedzadeh2026churn} reported its largest gain, serves only as a replication check (Section~\ref{sec:rq5}) and
is excluded from all cross-dataset summaries.

All models share one preprocessing pipeline. Categorical columns are ordinal-encoded with missing values left as
missing, and columns that are more than 99\% missing or constant are dropped. For Maven we drop customers whose status
is ``Joined'', whose outcome is not yet observed, and the two columns that record the churn reason after the fact. We
remove exact duplicate feature rows, keeping the first, because an in-context learner can otherwise find a test
customer's twin in its own context. This removed \dupTelco{} rows (\dupPctTelco\%) from Telco, \dupIranian{}
(\dupPctIranian\%) from Iranian and \dupECommerce{} (\dupPctECommerce\%) from E-Commerce; the other datasets had none.
The removal changed churn rates by at most \maxChurnShift{} points, and Section~\ref{sec:sens} repeats the analysis with duplicates
kept.

\subsection{Models}
\textbf{Foundation models.} TabPFN-2.5, TabPFN-3.5 and TabPFN-3.5-Fast run through the \texttt{tabpfn} package (v9.2.0)
with default settings and the training-size guardrail disabled; TabICL v2 runs through \texttt{tabicl} (v2.2.0,
checkpoint of 2026-02-12). Each receives the full training split as its context, at the natural churn rate. None is
tuned, which is how these models are meant to be used.

\textbf{Tuned baselines.} LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm} and XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost} are tuned with 40 trials
of Optuna's TPE sampler \cite{akiba2019optuna}, and CatBoost \cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost} with 25, each under a
20-minute cap. Trials are scored by validation AUC on an inner 80/20 split of the training data with early stopping;
the best configuration is then refit on the whole training split with the selected number of trees. Search spaces are
listed in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}. We also report an explainable boosting machine (EBM) \cite{nori2019interpretml} and
logistic regression with one-hot encoding and median imputation, both at defaults.

\subsection{Protocol and inference}\label{sec:inference}
For each dataset and each of \nSeeds{} seeds we draw a stratified 60/20/20 split into training, calibration and test
sets. All models see identical splits, which the released audit verifies. Models are fitted (or given context) on the
training split; the calibration split is used only to fit post-hoc recalibration; every reported metric is computed on
the test split. GBDTs ran on Kaggle CPU sessions and \tfms on a single NVIDIA T4 (16\,GB).

The estimand is the difference in a test-set metric between a \tfm and a baseline, averaged over random splits. The
\emph{primary family} is fixed in advance: EMP, each of the four \tfms against the best tuned GBDT on each of the five
main datasets, \nPrimary{} comparisons in all. The reference GBDT is chosen per dataset and metric as the tuned GBDT
with the best mean score on the calibration split, so the test set plays no part in choosing it. Two sensitivity
analyses address the remaining selection: choosing the reference by its test mean instead, and an
intersection--union test that requires a \tfm to beat each of the three tuned GBDTs separately, taking the largest of
the three $p$-values, again Holm-corrected over the \nPrimary{} comparisons. Because random splits share training and test customers, the seed-level differences
are correlated, and a plain $t$-test over seeds understates their variance. We therefore use the corrected resampled
$t$-test of Nadeau and Bengio \cite{nadeau2003inference}, which inflates the variance of the mean difference by
$1/J + n_\mathrm{test}/n_\mathrm{train}$ for $J$ splits, and apply Holm's correction \cite{holm1979simple} across the
primary family. We call a primary comparison significant when its Holm-adjusted $p$ is below 0.05, and report the
corrected test's 95\% confidence interval, which is unadjusted for multiplicity. A two-level bootstrap that resamples
seeds and then test customers is computed as a descriptive check; it does not model the overlap between splits, so it
is narrower than the corrected test, and no claim rests on it.
Everything outside the primary family, including PR-AUC, the ablations and the replication, is exploratory and reported
with unadjusted $p$.

\subsection{Metrics: three questions}
We keep three questions apart, because a model can answer one well and another badly.

\textbf{Does the model rank churners well?} ROC-AUC, PR-AUC and lift in the top decile.

\textbf{Does the ranking earn more campaign profit?} For a campaign that contacts the fraction of customers with the
highest scores, a contacted churner accepts the offer with probability $\gamma$ and is then worth
$\mathrm{CLV}-\delta-\phi$; a churner who declines costs the contact cost $\phi$; a contacted non-churner takes the
incentive and costs $\delta+\phi$. EMP is the expected profit per customer at the profit-maximising cutoff, averaged
over $\gamma\sim\mathrm{Beta}(6,14)$ \cite{verbraken2013emp}, with the standard $\mathrm{CLV}=200$, $\delta=10$ and
$\phi=1$. Because EMP picks the best cutoff for each model, it depends only on the ranking: a high EMP does not mean the
probabilities can be used directly. We also report e-Profits \cite{manzoor2025eprofits}, which values each customer as
$\mathrm{CLV}_i = R_i M/(1-\min(r_i,0.995))$ from monthly revenue $R_i$, margin $M=0.3$ and one-month retention
$r_i = S(T_i+1)/S(T_i)$ from a Kaplan--Meier curve $S$ \cite{kaplan1958nonparametric} fitted on the training split. The
offer costs $0.1\,\mathrm{CLV}_i$ and the contact $\max(5, 0.3\cdot\text{offer})$. We report it when contacting the top
20\% and, as in \cite{manzoor2025eprofits}, when contacting customers with predicted probability at least 0.5; the
latter mixes ranking with calibration.

\textbf{Can the probabilities drive a threshold policy?} An operator who acts on probabilities contacts customer $i$
when its expected profit under the EMP cost model is positive at the mean acceptance rate $\bar\gamma=0.3$. We report
the policy's regret: the profit lost relative to the best cutoff on the same ranking, as a share of that best profit,
as the median over a $3\times3$ grid of $\mathrm{CLV}\in\{100,200,500\}$ and $\delta\in\{5,10,20\}$. Calibration is
measured by expected calibration error (ECE, 15 equal-width bins), Brier score, and calibration-in-the-large: the
observed churn rate minus the mean predicted probability, in percentage points, positive when churn is underpredicted.
Each model is evaluated raw and after isotonic regression \cite{zadrozny2002transforming} or Platt scaling
\cite{platt1999probabilistic}, both fitted on the calibration split.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Do foundation models earn more profit than tuned GBDTs?}\label{sec:rq1}

TabPFN-3.5 earns significantly more EMP than the best tuned GBDT on two of five datasets (Table~\ref{tab:primary},
Figure~\ref{fig:forest}). On Cell2Cell the gain is \empAbsPFNThreeCellTwoCellAbs{} per customer
(\empPFNThreeCellTwoCellAbs\%; Holm $p$ \holmPFNThreeCellTwoCell), and on Iranian \empAbsPFNThreeIranianAbs{}
(\empPFNThreeIranianAbs\%; Holm $p$ = \holmPFNThreeIranian). On Iranian every \tfm is significantly ahead, which reflects
how far the boosted models fall behind there rather than anything specific to TabPFN. In total, \nHolmSig{} of the \nPrimary{} primary
comparisons are significant after correction, and none favours the GBDT. The selection of the reference does not
drive this: choosing it on test means instead gives \nHolmSigTest{} significant comparisons, and the stricter
intersection--union test against all three tuned GBDTs gives \nIUSig{} (\iuSigCases).

Orange is the case the correction decides. TabPFN-3.5 beats the best tuned GBDT in all \nSeeds{} splits, by
\empAbsPFNThreeOrangeAbs{} per customer, which is \empPFNThreeOrangeAbs\% of an EMP that is small because only
\rateOrange\% of Orange customers churn. Its corrected $p$ is \nbpPFNThreeOrange{} before adjustment and
\holmPFNThreeOrange{} after. We read this as a consistent gain whose size is not established; the relative figure in
particular should not be carried to other operators.

On the two fictional datasets no difference survives correction. TabPFN-3.5 is ahead by \empPFNThreeTelcoAbs\% on
Telco and \empPFNThreeMavenAbs\% on Maven, with Holm-adjusted $p$ of 1. TabICL v2 is significant only on Iranian, and on
Orange its mean EMP is below the GBDT's (\empICLOrange\%, not significant). Version matters more than family: TabPFN-2.5 and TabPFN-3.5 share a
lineage, yet only the latter separates from the GBDTs on Cell2Cell, which matches the pattern reported for credit risk
\cite{baesens2026creditrisk}. PR-AUC differences, treated as exploratory, point the same way (Appendix,
Table~\ref{tab:boot}).

\subsection{Do profit metrics change which model wins?}\label{sec:rq2}

They change it when models are tied and leave it alone otherwise (Table~\ref{tab:rank}). On Cell2Cell and Orange, where
TabPFN-3.5 leads clearly, the EMP winner differs from the AUC winner in only \topEMPCellTwoCell{} and \topEMPOrange{} of \nSeeds{} splits. On Telco and Maven,
where no difference is significant, the EMP winner differs from the AUC winner in \topEMPTelco{} and \topEMPMaven{} of
\nSeeds{} splits. Iranian sits in between (\topEMPIranian{} of \nSeeds): the four \tfms are tied with each other there,
though all lead the GBDTs.

e-Profits at the 0.5 threshold disagrees with AUC the most ($\tau=\tauEPthrMaven$ on Maven). This follows from its
construction rather than from a better ranking: at a fixed threshold the metric also rewards a model whose
probabilities happen to fall on the right side of 0.5. The reranking reported for e-Profits on Telco and Maven
\cite{manzoor2025eprofits} is therefore reproduced here, among models whose rankings are statistically
indistinguishable.

\subsection{Can the probabilities drive a threshold policy?}\label{sec:rq3}

For most models, yes. Outside Orange, the fixed-threshold policy loses at most \maxRawRegretNonOrange\% of attainable
profit without recalibration, and isotonic or Platt scaling changes that loss by at most \maxRegretShiftNonOrange{}
points (Table~\ref{tab:cal}).

Orange is the exception, and one model drives it. Raw TabICL v2 predicts \citlICLOrangeAbs{} percentage points too
little churn on a base rate of \rateOrange\% (Figure~\ref{fig:rel}), and the policy built on those probabilities loses
\regICLOrangeRaw\% of attainable profit. Isotonic regression cuts the loss to \regICLOrangeIso\% and Platt scaling to
\regICLOrangePlatt\%. TabICL v2 also underpredicts on Cell2Cell (by \citlICLCellTwoCellAbs{} points), as does TabPFN-2.5
(\citlPFNTwoCellTwoCellAbs). TabPFN-3.5's calibration-in-the-large stays within $\pm$\maxAbsCitlPFNThree{} points on
every dataset, so it is the only \tfm here whose raw probabilities can drive a threshold policy. The tuned GBDTs are
close to zero as well, consistent with their fitting to the observed base rate.

\subsection{Does a balanced context help?}\label{sec:rq4}

Under heavy imbalance, a balanced context has been reported to add 3 to 4 AUC points for \tfms
\cite{tanna2026resampling}. If that held here, our full-context runs would understate the \tfms on Orange. For
TabPFN-3.5 and TabICL v2 on Orange and Cell2Cell we kept every churner in the training split and sampled one or two
non-churners per churner, on the first five splits.

Balancing hurt (Table~\ref{tab:ctx}). TabPFN-3.5's EMP gain on Orange fell from \ctxOrangePFNFullAbs\% with the full
context to \ctxOrangePFNHalfAbs\% at 1:2 and \ctxOrangePFNBalAbs\% at 1:1 (unadjusted $p$ \ctxOrangePFNpHalf{} and
\ctxOrangePFNpBal). TabICL v2 gained nothing on either dataset. Balancing also inflated raw predicted churn, by
\ctxCitlOrangeAbs{} points on Orange at 1:1, which isotonic recalibration removes. The difference from
\cite{tanna2026resampling} is the regime: there, contexts were subsampled to at most 50k rows from larger data, so
balancing chose which customers to keep. Here the whole training split fits, and undersampling only discards
non-churners.

\subsection{Robustness: tuning budget and duplicates}\label{sec:sens}

\textbf{Tuning budget.} Our baselines use 25 to 40 trials, fewer than benchmark practice \cite{erickson2025tabarena}.
We reran LightGBM and CatBoost with 200 trials each on Cell2Cell, Iranian and Orange for the first five splits. The
larger budget changed the best GBDT's mean EMP by $\strongDeltaCellTwoCell\%$, $\strongDeltaIranian\%$ and
$\strongDeltaOrange\%$ respectively, and the \tfm gains were unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:sens}a). On Cell2Cell the
200-trial LightGBM becomes the reference and TabPFN-3.5 still gains \strongPFNThreeCellTwoCellAbs\% in each of the five splits.

\textbf{Duplicates.} Removing duplicates could discard legitimate customers. A duplicate group is a set of rows with identical
features; removal keeps one row per group, discarding the other copies. In E-Commerce every group's copies share the
same churn label. In Iranian, \conflictGroupsIranian{} groups (\conflictRowsIranian{} rows in total) and in Telco
\conflictGroupsTelco{} groups (\conflictRowsTelco{} rows) contain copies with different labels, so keeping the first
copy assigns them one label; removal changed churn rates by at most
\maxChurnShift{} points. We repeated the analysis keeping every row but assigning all copies of a feature vector to the same side of
each split, so that no twin crosses from context to test. The gains moved by at most \maxDupShift{} points on all three datasets
(Table~\ref{tab:sens}b).

We reran our protocol on E-Commerce, where \cite{seyedzadeh2026churn} reported TabICL v2 9.23 PR-AUC points ahead of

\subsection{Scoring cost and licensing}\label{sec:rq6}

An in-context learner re-reads its context for every scoring call unless the context is cached, so its cost depends on
how scoring is run. We timed scoring of 1k, 10k and 50k customers resampled from the test split, after a warm-up call,
on one T4 (Table~\ref{tab:time}). With the context cached, TabPFN-3.5 scores 10k Orange customers in
\timePFNCacheOrange{}\,s against a \ctxOrange-row context, and TabICL v2 with its key-value cache in
\timeICLCacheOrange{}\,s, against \timeLGBMOrange{}\,s for a 1,000-tree LightGBM on CPU. A monthly scoring run of a
million customers would therefore take about \hoursPFNCacheMillion{} T4-hours with cached TabPFN-3.5. This conclusion is
conditional on batch scoring, a cached context and this hardware; real-time serving with tight latency budgets is a
different question we did not measure. Without caching, small batches are very expensive
(\timePFNUncachedSmallOrange\,s per 10k for TabPFN-3.5 at 1k rows on Orange), and TabPFN-2.5 was impractical at
30k-row contexts.

The binding constraint is the licence. The TabPFN-3.5 and TabPFN-2.5 weights are released under non-commercial
licences that forbid using the model, its derivatives ``or any data produced by'' the model ``for any commercial or
production purposes'' \cite{tabpfn35license,tabpfn25license}; the licences define outputs to include predictions,
scores and probabilities. The TabPFN-3.5 report states the practical consequence: outputs may not be used ``as inputs
to internal commercial decision-making'' without a commercial licence \cite[Section~4]{priorlabs2026tabpfn35}. The
TabICL v2 weights are BSD-3 licensed \cite{tabiclweights}. These terms were read on 2026-10-28 and may change. An
operator therefore faces a trade-off that our results make concrete: the model with the most consistent gains needs a
commercial agreement, and the openly licensed one is significantly better than a tuned GBDT on only one of five
datasets.

\section{Discussion}

The results support a narrower statement than ``foundation models outperform trees for churn''. With tuned baselines
and a correction for multiple comparisons, one model generation, TabPFN-3.5, earns more campaign profit than a tuned
GBDT on two of five datasets and is never behind; the other \tfms are rarely distinguishable from the GBDT. Three
factors explain most of the variation. The model version matters more than the class. Baseline tuning removes part of
any measured lead, about a third on E-Commerce. And the datasets most used in churn research, Telco and Maven, are
those on which no model can be told apart, so studies built on them can neither confirm nor refute a \tfm advantage.

For a practitioner this suggests an order of operations. Tune the GBDT first; default settings overstate what a \tfm
adds. Evaluate candidates on held-out campaign profit, not only AUC. If TabPFN-3.5 still leads and its licence terms
are acceptable, score with a cached context. If only openly licensed models are possible, test TabICL v2 on one's own
data and recalibrate it before thresholding. A balanced context is not a free improvement when the training set fits.

The finding most likely to change with more evidence is the size of the Orange gain: it is consistent across splits
but not established after correction, and a feature-engineered GBDT \cite{tschalzev2026tabprep} could narrow it. The
finding least likely to change is the calibration pattern, which is consistent across splits and matches the
underprediction reported for credit risk \cite{baesens2026creditrisk}.

\section{Limitations}

The evidence rests on three real-operator datasets. With \nSeeds{} splits and a correction over \nPrimary{}
comparisons, the study has limited power: the Orange result is consistent in direction but not significant, and more
splits or datasets could move it either way. We cannot claim that TabPFN-3.5's gains generalise to churn in other
industries, or to operators with millions of customers, where the context would no longer hold the full training set.

The baselines were tuned with 25 to 40 trials; a fivefold budget changed nothing on three datasets, but we did no
feature engineering, which can shift model comparisons \cite{tschalzev2026tabprep}, and we did not record the selected
hyperparameters. Removing duplicate feature rows is a design choice; the grouped-split check covers it for the three
affected datasets. The ablations, the sensitivity checks and the replication use five splits.

The cost model behind EMP and the policy uses the standard population-level parameters \cite{verbraken2013emp};
e-Profits gives a customer-level alternative on three datasets only. We used one GPU type and the default inference
settings of each \tfm, and did not evaluate the API-only TabPFN-3.5-Plus and -Thinking variants or the other \tfms
covered by \cite{seyedzadeh2026churn}.

\section{Conclusion}

Measured with tuned baselines, in money, and with a correction for multiple comparisons, tabular foundation models pay
off for churn selectively. TabPFN-3.5 earns significantly more campaign profit than a tuned GBDT on Cell2Cell and
Iranian, wins in every split on Orange without surviving correction, and is never behind; its raw probabilities are
also the best calibrated. Its weights, however, cannot be used in production without a commercial licence. TabICL v2
and TabPFN-2.5 are rarely distinguishable from a tuned GBDT, and TabICL v2 needs recalibration before its probabilities
can drive a campaign. Profit metrics confirm clear winners and reshuffle ties. We release the pipeline and every
per-split prediction so that these conclusions can be re-checked as models and licences change.
